Ruffwear Front Range Flex Harness Review 2026: Does It Fit an English Bulldog's Wide Chest?

We checked the new knit harness against bulldog proportions, Ruffwear's size chart and 87 owner reviews.

Bulldog Guide Research Desk

Our Ruffwear Front Range Flex harness review starts with the verdict: the new knit Front Range Flex can fit an English Bulldog's wide chest, but the chest is rarely the problem. The fit usually hinges on the neck loop sliding over a big bulldog head and on a chest panel that some owners say runs longer than the original Front Range. Measure three places before you buy, not one.

Quick answer

Buy the Flex for a bulldog if you want the lightest everyday harness in the Front Range family and your dog's head passes comfortably through an opened neck loop. Pick the padded original Front Range if you want the familiar cut at a lower price. Look at the Ridgeline if you want a lifting handle, magnetic buckles and six adjustment points, and the $179.99 price does not put you off. All three use the same girth bands from XXS to L/XL.

Here is the context for those numbers. Ruffwear launched the Flex as part of its Spring/Summer 2026 line, and the launch press release published by pet-industry magazine PetsPlus describes it as an everyday harness made with an engineered knit "inspired by performance athletic footwear." The same release introduced the Ridgeline collection with Fidlock magnetic buckles and X-Pac RX30 fabric. So the "new knit harness" and the premium Ridgeline arrived in the same season.

Ruffwear's own short demo shows how the Flex adjusts, how the webbing length can be customized, and where the two leash points sit. Watch the neck loop as it goes on; that is the moment that matters most for a bulldog.

Introduction and first impressions: who the Flex is for

The Flex is a lighter, softer take on Ruffwear's best-selling Front Range, aimed at everyday walks rather than heavy-duty hauling. Ruffwear says the knit "conforms to and moves with a dog's body," and that it cuts rubbing and chafing. Those are maker claims, so we tested them against what owners actually wrote.

A straight word on how we built this review. We are a bulldog care desk, not a gear lab, and we have not had a Flex on our own bulldog for months. Instead we read the full spec sheets for all three harnesses, the AKC breed standard for bulldog body shape, and all 87 verified buyer reviews posted on Ruffwear's Flex page between late February and October 2026. Where an owner of a broad, short-necked dog spoke up, we quote them. Where we could not confirm something, we say so.

That approach turned up something the product photos hide. The Flex reviews that mention trouble almost never complain about girth. They talk about neck loops, chest panel length and size swaps. That is exactly where bulldogs differ from the greyhounds, Labs and terriers who fill most of the review pages.

Front Range Flex specifications, sizing and price

The Flex comes in six sizes, from XXXSmall (9-13 inch girth) to Large/XLarge (32-42 inch girth), and listed at $69.99 on ruffwear.com in October 2026. Here is what Ruffwear lists on the official Front Range Flex product page:

  • Body: 75% polyester / 25% nylon engineered knit.
  • Webbing: 100% polyester, 30% recycled.
  • Leash points: an anodized aluminum V-ring on the back and reinforced nylon webbing in molded TPU at the chest.
  • Fit: four points of adjustment and ITW Nexus side-release buckles.
  • Extras: reflective trim, a light loop for The Beacon light, an ID pocket for tags.
  • Care: cold-water gentle wash, mild detergent, air dry. Made in Vietnam.

What is in the box

Just the harness. There are no spare straps and no sizing inserts. Ruffwear sells a matching Flex collar and Flex leash on their own, so budget separately if you want the set.

Target audience

Ruffwear pitches the Flex at "companions of all breeds and sizes" and at high-energy outings. For bulldog homes, the honest fit is shorter, everyday walks where a light, quick-drying harness is a plus. If you are still working on loose-leash basics, pair the harness with the steps in our guide to a bulldog puppy who plants and won't walk on a leash.

Design and build quality: knit versus foam versus X-Pac

The three harnesses share a family name but use very different materials. The Flex is knit, the original Front Range is foam-padded, and the Ridgeline uses a laminated X-Pac shell.

Looks and feel

The Flex has a slim, low profile. Owners describe it as "trim" and "minimalist." One reviewer with a narrow coonhound liked that it fit "so much better" than "the standard front range." That same slimness is worth a pause for a bulldog. The AKC standard calls for a chest that is "very broad, deep and full" and shoulders that are "muscular, very heavy, widespread." A slim panel can still work on that frame, but you need to check where the straps land.

Materials and durability

Ruffwear lists PE foam padding for the original Front Range and notes its latest version added "improvements for easier adjustment on girth straps." The Ridgeline uses X-Pac RX30, which the PetsPlus release calls a 100% waterproof laminate.

The Flex durability record from owners is mixed but mostly positive. A verified buyer with a 70-pound puller said the construction will "hold up," noted a plastic sleeve over the front loop that cuts wear, and then said the upper Y "bunches and frequently requires being shifted." The French Bulldog owner in the reviews reported a strap snapping at the buckle on one harness and the buckle itself breaking on the next. One story is not a trend, but it came from the dog shape closest to ours.

Ergonomics

Most owners say the knit is easy to adjust. One owner of a "50lb american bully" who wears size Small wrote that the standard Front Range "would chafe his sensitive soft belly, but the Flex is not causing that issue." Another said it gets "softer the more we use it." Those are the comments that make the Flex tempting for a stocky dog.

How the Front Range Flex fits an English Bulldog: performance analysis

For a bulldog, "performance" means three things: does it fit the girth, does it go on over the head, and does it sit right on a short body. Leash pulling comes fourth.

Girth: usually fine

The AKC standard puts a mature bulldog at about 50 pounds for males and about 40 pounds for females. A barrel chest is exactly what girth-based sizing is built for. As long as you measure behind the front legs, at the widest point, and round up, the band will close.

Head versus neck: the real test

This is where the Flex gets tricky for bulldogs. The AKC Bulldog breed standard (PDF) says the skull "in front of the ears, should measure at least the height of the dog at the shoulders," and that the neck should be "short, very thick, deep and strong." So the head is big and the neck is short.

Now the review that every bulldog owner should read. A verified buyer gave the Flex one star because it has no clip around the neck: "if your dogs head is wider then their neck the harness strap around the head has to be adjusted and made bigger to take the harness off," and then tightened again on the next walk. The reviewer called it good for "a dog like a greyhound." Ruffwear's spec sheet says only "side-release buckles for easy on/off" and does not say where they sit, so check the neck loop yourself the day it arrives.

Our practical take: if your bulldog's head measurement is clearly bigger than the neck, plan on one of two routines. Loosen the neck strap before every walk, or keep the neck loop loose and get the snug fit from the girth straps. If that sounds like a daily fight, the Flex is the wrong harness for your dog.

Chest panel length on a short body

Two owners flagged the chest panel. One said it is "more than one size longer" than on the regular Front Range, and that a 28-pound dog could not size down in Flex to fix it. Another said the longer chest piece "gaps a bit" on a Boston Terrier. A third, with a beagle, found the Flex XS "much smaller" around the chest than the Front Range XS.

Bulldogs are short-coupled. A panel that is long for a Boston may press toward the girth strap or bunch up on a bulldog too. That is not proven for every bulldog, but it is the second thing to check during a fitting.

Leash pulling

The Flex uses the same two-point idea as the original: a back V-ring for normal walking and a front webbing loop to "redirect dogs that pull on leash." A Lab owner said the front hook slowed the dog down "by about 70%." That is one owner's estimate, not a measurement, but it shows the front point is the reason many people buy it.

User experience: putting it on, daily use and washing

Day to day, the Flex is quick to clip on, light to wear and easy to wash, once you have dialed in the neck strap.

Setup

Set the fit once with all four adjusters, with your bulldog standing square. Leave room for two flat fingers under each strap. Mark the neck strap position with a tiny dot of fabric pen so you can return to it if you loosen it to get the harness off.

Daily use

Owners like the low weight. One said the Flex is "lighter than the frontline but a little heavier than the lite harness," and that it is "a little wide in chest." Another said a Front Range Flex was "the only one" a nervous dog would accept after more than ten other harnesses. Light gear is nice on warm days, but it does not change how long your walks should be.

Learning curve

The buckles are standard side-release clips, so there is nothing to learn. The real learning curve is yours: finding the neck setting that passes the head without leaving the harness loose. If your bulldog is also learning to handle a new routine, short, rewarded practice works the same way we describe for calmer bulldog nail trimming.

Washing

Ruffwear says cold gentle cycle and air dry. An owner of an American Staffordshire Terrier wrote that they were "constantly removing hair buildup from the belly area of the original harness" and find "little to none" on the Flex. For a short-coated breed that sheds, that is a small daily win.

When the original Front Range wins

It costs $10 less, has years of track record and, by Ruffwear's own review summary, "multiple adjustment points" that suit "various dog shapes." That same summary notes "a few customers" wish for "easier neck adjustments." If your bulldog already wears a Front Range happily, the Flex is a comfort upgrade, not a fit fix.

When the Ridgeline wins

Ruffwear lists six adjustment points, "a load-dispersing chest and belly panel," a padded handle and magnetic buckles that work "with just one hand." For a bulldog who needs a boost into the car or over a step, the handle is the real feature. Pair it with the tips in our guide on whether bulldogs should use stairs. One Ridgeline buyer with an "XL Bully" said it was a "great fit." Another, with a Lab, said the top edge of the collar section "has the potential of rub."

When the Flex wins

Pick it for warm-weather walks, for a dog who dislikes bulky gear, and for owners who hate hair and grime in foam. It is also the only one of the three with an XXXS size, which matters if you also own a tiny dog.

Pros and cons for bulldog owners

What we loved

  • Very light, soft knit that owners say gets softer with use.
  • Same simple girth bands as the rest of the family, so sizing a bulldog is straightforward.
  • A front leash point for dogs who pull, plus an aluminum back V-ring.
  • Easy machine wash and a high share of 5-star reviews: 70 of 87 on Ruffwear's site.

Areas for improvement

  • No buckle on the neck loop, according to a verified buyer, which is awkward for wide-headed dogs.
  • Chest panel runs longer than the original, per several owners, so short bodies may see gaps or bunching.
  • Sizing is not always the same as your dog's Front Range size; one owner had to reorder.
  • One French Bulldog owner reported two buckle or strap failures; small sample, but worth knowing.
  • No handle. If you lift your bulldog, look at the Ridgeline instead.

Evolution: what changed from the original Front Range

The Flex keeps the Front Range layout and swaps cut-and-sew foam for a single engineered knit. Ruffwear says the knit process "reduces material waste compared to standard cut-and-sew harness construction." It also added the XXXS size.

The original Front Range itself was updated with easier girth-strap adjustment and more recycled material in the shell, webbing and lining, so you are comparing against a refreshed version, not an old one. There is no published roadmap for a Flex with a neck buckle. If Ruffwear adds one, that single change would make this a much easier pick for bulldogs.

Purchase recommendations and where to buy

Best for

  • Bulldogs whose head slides through the opened neck loop without a struggle.
  • Owners who walk in warm months and want something light and quick to dry.
  • Dogs who rub or fuss in padded harnesses.

Skip if

  • Your bulldog's head is much bigger than the neck and you do not want to re-adjust daily.
  • You need a handle to help your dog up or down.
  • Your dog is a hard lunger; one reviewer with a 70-pound puller had to hand-stitch the webbing to stop twisting.

Alternatives to consider

The original Front Range for value and padding. The Ridgeline for a handle, magnetic buckles and six adjustment points. For other needs, look for any harness with a buckle on the neck loop, which removes the over-the-head step entirely.

Where to buy and what to watch for

Ruffwear sells direct and through "select retail partners." Buy from a shop with an easy exchange policy, since swapping sizes is common with this model. One owner said Ruffwear "made a return very easy." Keep the tags on until you have tried the harness indoors and walked your dog around the house. Price the harness into your gear line using our annual bulldog budget planner, and remember a good fit is worth more than a sale price.

Final verdict: Front Range Flex harness rating for English Bulldogs

Our research rating for English Bulldogs: 3.5 out of 5. As a harness, the Flex is light, soft and well reviewed; owners rate it 4.7 on average. As a bulldog harness, it loses points for the over-the-head neck loop and the longer chest panel. Neither is a deal-breaker for every dog, but both hit bulldogs harder than most breeds.

Bottom line: measure girth, head and neck before you order. If the head is close to the neck size, the Ruffwear Front Range Flex harness is a comfortable everyday pick for an English Bulldog's wide chest. If not, the original Front Range or the Ridgeline will save you a daily wrestle.

Frequently asked questions

What size Front Range Flex does an English Bulldog need?

Measure the girth around the widest part of the rib cage and match it to Ruffwear's bands: Small 22-27 in, Medium 27-32 in, Large/XLarge 32-42 in. Between sizes, take the larger one. Weight alone is not a reliable guide.

Does the Front Range Flex have a neck buckle?

A verified buyer reported there is no clip around the neck, so the loop goes over the head and may need loosening for wide-headed dogs. Ruffwear's spec lists side-release buckles without saying where they sit, so check this the day it arrives.

Is the Flex the same size as my dog's original Front Range?

Not always. Owners reported a longer chest panel on the Flex and, in one case, a tighter chest in the same size. Try it on indoors before removing tags.

Flex or Ridgeline for a bulldog?

Pick the Ridgeline if you want a padded handle, magnetic buckles and six adjustment points. Pick the Flex if you want the lightest everyday harness and do not need to lift your dog.

How much does the Front Range Flex cost?

It listed at $69.99 on ruffwear.com in October 2026, compared with $59.99 for the original Front Range and $179.99 for the Ridgeline. Retailer prices vary.

Can I machine wash the Flex?

Yes. Ruffwear says to secure the fasteners, wash in cold water on a gentle cycle with mild detergent and air dry.

Does the front leash point stop pulling?

It is designed to redirect dogs that pull. Owners report less pulling, but it is a training aid, not a fix on its own. Pair it with short reward-based practice.
